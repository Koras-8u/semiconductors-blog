Solid State Physics

Waves

Waves can be defined as self-propagating periodic disturbances that transport energy and momentum with no net transport of matter.

Wave equation

A wave equation is any partial differential equation that has solutions of the form \begin{equation} u(\vec{r},t) = A \sin \varphi \end{equation} or \begin{equation} u(\vec{r},t) = Ae^{i \varphi} \end{equation}

  • \( u \) can be any physical parameter that can display wave properties (the magnitude of the electric field of a light wave).

Phase

The phase or phase angle \( \varphi \) is an angular variable which defines the periodicity of a wave \begin{align} \varphi & = \vec{k} \cdot \vec{r} - \omega t \\ & = k_x x + k_y y + k_z z - \omega t \end{align}

  • \( \vec{k} \) is referred to as the propagation vector along an arbitrary direction in space (or as the propagation constant when the wave propagates along one of the coordinate axes).
    • Establishes the direction and the speed of the wave travel.
    • Can be written as \( \vec{k} = \nabla \varphi = k_x \hat{\imath} + k_y \hat{\jmath} + k_z \hat{k} \), where \( k_x \), \( k_y \) and \( k_z \) are the components of the propagation vector along the coordinate axes.
      • The direction of the gradient is that in which the phase \( \varphi \) advances most rapidly as a function of distance.
      • The magnitude \( k = \sqrt {k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2} \) is the rate of change of the phase angle \( \varphi \) along the direction of the gradient.
    • \( k = 2 \pi / \lambda \) means that, at any fixed time, if the wave advances a distance equal to the wavelength \( \lambda \), the phase angle \( \varphi \) must change by \( 2 \pi \).
    • If it is negative (\( -\vec{k} \)), reverses the direction of the wave.
    • If it is complex, the wave is exponentially attenuated, where the imaginary part plays the role of an exponential attenuating factor.
  • \( \omega \) is the angular frequency in \( [rad/s] \).
    • Always regarded as a real and positive quantity.
    • Related to the linear frequency by \( \omega = 2 \pi f \).
    • \( \omega = 2 \pi/T \) means that, at any fixed position, when a time equal to the period \( T \) of a single oscillation elapses, the phase angle must also change by \( 2\pi \).
    • If the oscillations are in the direction of propagation, the wave is longitudinal (e.g. sound waves).
    • If the oscillations are normal to the propagation direction, the wave is transverse (e.g. electromagnetic waves).

Phase velocity

The velocity of propagation or phase velocity can be visualized as the speed at which the crests and throughs of the wave advance. \begin{equation} v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} \end{equation} This is valid for both cases when the wave propagates along an arbitrary direction in space and when it moves along a specific coordinate axis.
It comes from tracking a single point \(s\) of the wave from a starting phase position \( s_0 \), which leads to \begin{align} s & = \frac{\omega}{k} t + \frac{\varphi _0}{k} \\ & = v_p t + s_0 \end{align}

Dispersion relation

Provides information about how waves propagate in a medium. Relation between the wavelength of a wave to its frequency, which is not always linear: \( \omega(k) \).

A wave propagating along the x-direction

The most common - and simplest - wave equation has the form \begin{equation} \frac{\partial ^2 u}{\partial x^2} = K^2 \frac{\partial ^2u}{\partial t^2} \end{equation} For a wave propagating along the x-direction. By calculating the first partial derivative for both \(x\) and \(t\), we get \begin{align} \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} & = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \right) \\ & = ik Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \end{align} \begin{align} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} & = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \right) \\ & = -i\omega Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \end{align} Then, we calculate both second partial derivatives \begin{align} \frac{\partial ^2 u}{\partial x^2} & = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( ik Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \right) \\ & = (ik)ik Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \\ & = -k^2 Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \end{align} \begin{align} \frac{\partial ^2 u}{\partial t^2} & = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( -i\omega Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \right) \\ & = (-i\omega)(-i\omega) Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \\ & = - \omega ^2 Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \end{align} Substituting in the wave equation \begin{align} -k^2 Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} & = - \omega ^2 K^2 Ae^{i (kx - \omega t)} \\ -k^2 & = - \omega ^2 K^2 \end{align} Which leads to \begin{equation} \omega = \frac{k}{K} \end{equation} From here we can conclude that \( \omega \propto k \) and \( \omega/k \) has the constant value of \( 1/K \) independent of frequency or wavelength.
Electromagnetic waves in free space obey wave equations of this form, in which \( K^2 \) has the value of \( \epsilon _0 \mu _0 \) giving a phase velocity \begin{equation} v_p = c = \frac{1}{ \sqrt{\epsilon _0 \mu _0} } \end{equation}
