The quantum harmonic oscillator

Simple harmonic motion is always associated with a potential energy varying as the square of the distance from an equilibrium point of minimum energy.
The classical potential energy of a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator is $ \beta x^2/2 $ where $ \beta $ is the force constant and $ x $ the displacement. It has the form of a parabolic well of inifnite depth.
Classically, a particle of mass $m$ under the influence of this potential oscillates with a constant frequency $\omega$ equal to $\sqrt{\beta/m}$.
The classical potential energy can thus be written as \begin{equation} V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\beta x^2 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2 \end{equation} which is inserted into the Schrödinger's equation \begin{equation} \frac{d^2 \psi}{d x^2}+\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left[\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2} m \omega^2 x^2\right] \psi(x)=0 \end{equation} The eigenfunctions for this differential equation happens to be \begin{equation} \psi_n(\xi)=N_n H_n(\xi) e^{-\xi^2 / 2} \end{equation} with $\xi = x\sqrt{\alpha}$, $n= 0, 1, 2, 3, ...$ and \begin{equation} N_n^2=\frac{1}{2^n n!} \sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}} \end{equation} where $N_n^2$ is the normalization constant, chosen to satisfy condition, and $H_n(\xi)$ represents the Hermite polynomials.
The energy eigenvalues ${\varepsilon_n}$ must be given \begin{equation} \varepsilon_n = \left( n + \frac{1}{2} \right) \hbar \omega \end{equation}
